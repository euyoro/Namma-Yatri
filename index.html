What I Was Given

The brief asked for frictionless. I had to figure out what was causing the friction.

Citizens encounter potholes, broken streetlights, garbage dumps, water leaks. Existing apps are slow, confusing, and opaque. Namma Yatri wanted a hyper-simplified reporting and tracking flow for a public citizen mobile app.

Four days. Complete creative freedom. One constraint: make it frictionless and trustworthy.

"The experience should empower citizens to report a public utility issue the moment they encounter it, and transparently track its progress through to resolution." — Namma Yatri Assignment Brief

I spent day one not in Figma. I needed to understand why that brief existed before I could solve it.

What's Actually Broken

"Resolved" doesn't mean fixed.

I dug into app store reviews, news reports, Reddit threads, and public case filings. What I found was consistent across every platform I looked at.

In November 2024, the Times of India reported Bengaluru residents finding Sahaaya tickets marked "resolved" within hours. Sometimes with blank images attached. The pothole was still there. Hindustan Times independently filed 6 garbage complaints. All 6 were marked resolved within 24 hours. All 6 locations were still dirty.

"It's all fake. MCD is closing complaints from the backend without any resolution or intimation. You cannot even reopen the ticket within 24 hours."

App Store review, Swachhata-MoHUA

This is a data architecture problem wearing a UX mask. The system has a status = RESOLVED field. It doesn't have a physical_world_state = ACTUALLY_FIXED field. Those are two different variables. The app can work perfectly while the service-delivery network behind it is completely broken.

// What the current data model tracks
{ status: "RESOLVED" }

// What it's missing
{ physical_world_state: "ACTUALLY_FIXED" }

I found two more failures underneath this one.

Routing failures: in the 2025 Bengaluru footpath case, a complaint got assigned to several departments simultaneously. None claimed ownership. The citizen made 15–20 calls before anyone acted. GBA's grievance backlog hit 25,000 complaints in December 2025.

Submission failures: OTP loops, image upload restrictions, crash cycles. The reporting channel broke before citizens could report anything. When the effort to report exceeds the motivation to report, people stop.

Problem A

"Resolved" is a ticket state. It's not a physical state.

Officers can close tickets without fixing anything. No citizen confirmation exists in any of the five major apps I studied.

Problem B

Routing breaks at department handoff

When jurisdiction is unclear, nobody acts. The citizen absorbs the cost of the system's internal confusion.

Problem C

The channel kills intent before submission

Swachhata's App Store rating is 1.9. BBMP Fix My Street users report 10-minute load times. The first friction point isn't the form. It's the app opening.

Problem D

Officers are incentivised to close tickets, not fix things

KPIs measure ticket closure speed. Changing the metric changes the behaviour. The design had to change the metric.

What's Already Out There

Five apps. The same structural gap in all of them.

I tore down every major Indian civic complaint app before touching a wireframe. I went beyond the App Store descriptions and looked at what users reported happened to them.

App What it does Where it fails
Swachhata-MoHUA The flagship. Photo + location routes to city corporation then sanitary inspector. Complaints marked resolved with fake images. App crashes on older Android. Phone numbers get blocked after repeated filing.
BBMP Fix My Street Android-only. GPS pothole reporting. Originally an internal engineer tool, opened to public. 10-minute load times, crash loops, no follow-up. Most users have abandoned it.
EyesCount AI visual classification, interactive city map, verification photos required for closure. Most sophisticated of the five. Geotags to precise location. Visible to elected officials. Closest to the right model.
i-Witness AI detection, GPS tagging, duplicate detection, community upvoting for severity. Good instincts. Community participation on the right path. Limited coverage area.
MyNeta Routes complaints to the citizen's local elected representative. Good for escalation. Built for civic pressure, not daily operational reporting.

All five are designed around the lifecycle of a ticket. They need to be designed around the lifecycle of a physical condition. That's the unlock.

The Structural Gap

I went one layer below the UX to find out what the system could actually know.

A civic incident generates three types of data: citizen data (photo, category, GPS, description, timestamp), system context (weather, rainfall, nearby assets, geography, historical incidents), and government data (officer details, work order IDs, resolution evidence). These combine into a unified incident record that moves through six stages: citizen app submission, backend geo-resolution, department routing, field assignment, field update, and citizen confirmation.

That last stage doesn't exist in any of the five apps I studied. The flow ends at field update. The officer marks it resolved. The ticket closes. Nobody asks the citizen.

Data structure diagram
The 6-stage flow from citizen submission to physical resolution. Stage 6 is the one that's missing.

The fix isn't a design fix alone. It's a data model extension: add citizen_verification_state, physical_resolution_confirmed, and reopen_count to the backend schema. The design makes that extension visible to the citizen. The citizen becomes a participant in resolution, not just a recipient of a status notification.

Who I Designed For

Three people. One broken system. Different stakes in fixing it.

Civic apps don't have a single user. They have a spectrum. I built three personas to represent that range, then tested every design decision against all three.

👩‍💼
Ananya Sharma, 27
Marketing Executive · Koramangala · High digital literacy
"I'll report it if it takes two minutes. I won't spend twenty minutes on a pothole I pass every day."
Her problem: Speed. She knows the system exists. She won't tolerate a flow that takes longer than her commute window.
👨‍💼
Ramesh Kumar, 42
Small Business Owner · Bengaluru · Moderate literacy
"Submitting is easy. Finding out who's actually going to fix it — that's the problem."
His problem: Accountability. He filed three complaints. Nothing happened. He calls manually now because the app gave him no visibility.
👩
Meena Devi, 35
Homemaker · Mysuru · Low-moderate literacy
"Ten people in our area have the same problem. Why do we each have to complain separately?"
Her problem: Collective voice. She discovers civic issues through neighbourhood groups. She wants the system to reflect that.

Ananya's problem is speed. Ramesh's problem is trust. Meena's problem is accessibility and collective representation. I didn't average them. I built a system that addresses each one on their own terms.

The Reframe

The brief said "reporting." The real problem was the loop that never closes.

Reporting is already simple enough in most apps. What destroys trust is everything after submission. The citizen files. The ticket closes. They never hear back. They stop filing.

I reframed the scope.

How might we make it effortless for citizens to report public infrastructure issues, while giving them the transparency to see those issues through to real, verified resolution?

Two words carried the most weight: effortless for Ananya, and real resolution for Ramesh and Meena. Those became the filter for every screen that followed.

Five Calls I Made

Every design decision I'd defend in a room.

Decision 01

Camera-first or category-first as the reporting entry point?

Chosen — dual path
Camera dominant, category available

Camera-first at 80% visual weight. Category grid below it. Both paths converge at the same review screen.

Rejected — camera only
Single entry point for everyone

Cleaner on paper. Forces a new mental model on users already trained by Swachhata's category-first pattern, which has 8.4M downloads.

Why: Swachhata has 8.4M downloads. Its users are trained on category-first. Forcing relearning adds friction in the first three seconds, exactly where drop-off is highest. Camera-first serves Ananya. Category is the fallback for Ramesh and Meena.
Decision 02

Map or list as the home screen's primary view?

Chosen
Map fills the top half, list pulls up below

Heatmap shows issue density. Bottom sheet slides up with the recent activity list. Toggle between Active and Resolved views.

Rejected
Pure list, map as secondary

Safer technically. Misses the product's core value: situational awareness. A list doesn't tell you your street has 12 open complaints.

Why: Meena's need is neighbourhood visibility. A heatmap answers "what's happening around me" without any reading required. Visual density communicates severity to low-literacy users faster than any text label.
Decision 03

Show only active issues, or resolved ones too?

Chosen
Toggle between Active and Recently Resolved

Two map states. Active uses the red/yellow heat gradient. Resolved uses green. Before/after evidence visible on tap.

Rejected
Active issues only

Standard for civic apps. Creates a complaint graveyard. Every screen reinforces what's wrong. No positive signal reaches the user.

Why: Trust is built by seeing the loop close. Swachhata's reviews say "nobody is listening." Showing resolutions with before/after evidence creates the incentive to report again. The app's best marketing is a fixed pothole.
Decision 04

What happens when the issue already has an open ticket?

Chosen
Show the ticket, offer to add evidence

System checks for existing issues after capture. Strongly surfaces the existing ticket. Creating a new one stays available.

Rejected
Always create a new ticket

Simpler to implement. Generates duplicate data, splits community weight, reduces the pressure that makes officials act.

Why: A pothole with 12 reports gets prioritised differently than one with 1. Evidence grouping solves Meena's "why do we all complain separately" at the system level. Collective weight is the product.
Decision 05

Who gets to decide if an issue is actually resolved?

Chosen
The citizen confirms, disputes, or reopens

When an officer marks resolved, the citizen gets notified. They view the evidence and choose: Approve, Partially Resolved, or Still Unresolved. Unresolved auto-reopens the ticket.

Rejected
Officer resolution is final

The current model on every existing app. Fast to implement. The root cause of the trust collapse the brief is trying to fix.

Why: This is the data architecture gap I found in secondary research. Adding citizen verification changes the incentive structure entirely. Officers can't close tickets arbitrarily if citizens reopen them. The metric shifts from ticket-closed to citizen-confirmed.
Three Flows

Report. Track. Verify. The full loop, not just the submission.

Most civic apps have one flow: submit and forget. I designed three, because submission is where civic trust begins. Verification is where it's earned.

Flow 01 — Reporting an issue

1
Entry: camera or category

Dual-path entry. Camera opens for speed. Category grid available for orientation-first users.

2
System captures GPS, timestamp, image metadata

Auto-populated. The user doesn't type their location. AI attempts category detection from the image and pre-fills the form.

3
Duplicate check

If an existing ticket exists nearby, the system surfaces it and strongly recommends adding evidence. New ticket remains available.

4
Review, confirm, done

One review screen. One CTA. Ticket ID generated. User lands back on home with their report in the list.

Flow 01 — user flow diagram
Full box-and-arrow flow for reporting, including gallery path, duplicate detection branch, and edge cases

Flow 02 — Tracking an issue

The ticket detail screen is where Ramesh lives. A status timeline shows every update: when it was assigned, which officer, what action was logged. When a ticket goes overdue, two options appear at the bottom: escalate to a higher authority, or contact support directly. No more 15 calls.

Flow 02 — tracking flow diagram
My Issues → Ticket Detail → Status Timeline → Overdue branch → Escalation and Resolution paths

Flow 03 — Verifying resolution

This is the flow no existing app has. When an officer marks a ticket resolved and uploads evidence, the citizen gets a notification. They see before/after photos and make one of three choices: Approve (closes the ticket with their confirmation), Partially Resolved (describes what remains), or Still Unresolved (reopens the ticket and notifies the authority).

No response within 72 hours triggers a reminder. After another 48 hours, the ticket auto-closes. The citizen can reopen it within 7 days. That window is the accountability lever.

The Screens

Four screens. Each one earns its place with a decision behind it.

The brief asked for 3–4 high-fidelity screens. I picked the four that carry the most product thinking.

Home — the map with a resolved toggle

The map fills the top half. A heatmap overlay shows issue density: yellow to red for active issues, green gradient for resolved. A two-state toggle at the top switches between Active and Recently Resolved views. Below the map, a bottom sheet pulls up with the recent activity list and category filter chips.

The resolved view is the trust signal. The app surfaces proof that problems get fixed, not just proof that they exist.

Screen 01 — Home
Map with heatmap, Active/Resolved toggle, bottom sheet with filter chips and issue cards

Heat encoding follows universal conventions: yellow to red for active density, green gradient for resolved. Colour is never the only signal — status labels appear on every issue card.

Report — dual path entry

The Report tab opens to a large camera CTA with a "Choose Category" option below it. Camera triggers capture immediately. The system auto-detects the issue category and pre-fills the form. The user can override the detection before submitting. Gallery images require a valid geotag and timestamp in the metadata. Images without either get rejected with a plain-language explanation.

Screen 02a — Report entry
Camera CTA dominant. Category option below.
Screen 02b — Duplicate detection
"We found a similar report nearby." Add evidence or create new ticket.

Ticket detail — status and timeline

The ticket detail shows the current status badge (Open, In Progress, Overdue, Resolved), the assigned officer's name when available, and a full chronological timeline of every update. Status uses both colour and label. When the issue goes overdue, "Escalate" and "Contact Support" appear at the bottom of the screen.

Screen 03 — Ticket detail
Status badge, officer info, full timeline, escalation CTAs

Verify resolution — the citizen's confirmation

The notification arrives. The ticket opens to before/after evidence submitted by the field officer. Three choices, clearly labeled. Tapping "Still Unresolved" reopens the ticket, notifies the authority, and timestamps the original report to establish how long this has been unresolved. Officers can't arbitrarily close tickets when citizens reopen them. That's the incentive shift this design depends on.

What I Scoped Out and Why

I made explicit scope decisions. Designing everything at 30% depth is worse than designing four things at depth.

In scope
Citizen verification of resolution. This is the structural fix the brief is actually asking for, even if it doesn't name it. Without it, the new app has the same architecture as every app that failed before it.
In scope
Duplicate detection and evidence grouping. Multiple reports on one ticket build pressure. This is Meena's "collective reporting" problem solved at the system level.
In scope
Active/Resolved toggle on map. The trust signal most civic apps miss. Resolution proof, not just complaint recording.
Deferred to V2
WhatsApp reporting. The integration requires separate backend work and a different onboarding flow. High priority for V2, especially for Meena, who already operates through WhatsApp groups.
Assumption
Multi-language UI. Typography uses Noto Sans, which covers all Indian scripts. The system is script-ready. Language selection on first launch is a handoff requirement, not a design deliverable here.
Won't have
Community issue map as a social feature, AI assistant for query handling, predictive maintenance, emergency services. These belong in the platform layer, not the MVP citizen flow.
Assumption
The backend can be extended to include citizen verification state and physical resolution confirmation. Without this schema change, Flow 03 can't be built. That's a discovery conversation with engineering. It's not a blocker to designing it.
What Ships, What Waits

MoSCoW applied. The Must Haves all trace back to one problem: the loop that never closes.

Must Have — V1

01

Camera-first reporting with GPS and timestamp auto-capture

Submission friction must stay below motivation to report. Camera-first with auto-populated location is how.

02

Duplicate detection and community evidence grouping

Prevents database noise. Builds collective weight. Solves Meena's question at the system level.

03

Status tracking with full update timeline

Ramesh's question — who is actually going to fix this — needs an answer on screen. A phone number to call is not an answer.

04

Citizen verification of resolution with reopen capability

The structural fix. Changes the incentive model. Without it, this is another Swachhata.

Should Have — V2

Smart department routing, expected resolution time, escalation to elected officials, WhatsApp reporting channel, community upvoting for severity, citizen feedback loop on officer performance.

Could Have — V3+

Community issue map as social layer, AI assistant, impact summary reports for residents, worker recognition system.

Won't Have — this cycle

Full authority dashboard, contractor management, predictive maintenance, emergency services, social network features, reward economy.

How I'd Measure This

Six numbers. Most civic apps track one of them.

Most civic apps measure submission volume. That's a vanity metric when the issues don't get fixed. I'd measure the full loop.

<3 min
Submission time
App open to confirmed ticket. Over three minutes means the flow has failed Ananya.
VRR ↑
Verified resolution rate
Tickets confirmed resolved by citizens, not officers. The metric none of the five competitor apps track.
DR ↓
Dispute rate
% of "resolved" tickets reopened by citizens. Tracks officer accuracy. Should trend down as incentives shift.
D30 ↑
30-day retention
Users who submit more than one report. Trust is built by seeing the loop close once.
EGR ↑
Evidence grouping rate
% adding to existing tickets rather than creating duplicates. Measures whether community behaviour is taking hold.
SCR ↓
Support contact rate
Users who contact support after filing. Should drop as status visibility removes the need to chase manually.

The north star is verified resolution rate: tickets actually fixed, confirmed by the person who reported them. Every other metric is a leading indicator for that number.

What's Next

Four things I'd do before this ships.

01

Test the verification flow with real users before assuming it works

The citizen verification step is the most consequential decision in this submission. I haven't tested it. I'd run five scenario-based interviews with Bengaluru residents, specifically around the "Still Unresolved" path. I need to know whether citizens feel comfortable pushing back against official resolutions, or whether social pressure makes them default to Approve.

02

Get backend engineering alignment on the schema extension

The current backend has status = RESOLVED. It needs citizen_verification_state, physical_resolution_confirmed, and reopen_count. This is a discovery sprint with the backend team. The design is ready. The schema needs a decision.

03

Design the officer-side experience

I designed the citizen side. The officer app is where behaviour change actually happens. Officers upload evidence. Officers mark resolution. Without designing that side, the citizen-verification loop is incomplete. That's a separate project with its own UX complexity and its own resistance to navigate.

04

Stress-test the heatmap on low-end devices and 2G connections

Heatmaps with live data are computationally expensive. The home screen needs a fallback state: a list-first view that activates when the map takes more than three seconds to load. I'd design and test that fallback before shipping to a market where mid-range Android and patchy connectivity are the norm.

What working on this taught me

I came in thinking the problem was UX. Citizens find the apps confusing. The flow is long. The buttons are small. Fix that and people report more.

That's not wrong. It's upstream of the real issue.

People stopped reporting because they filed three complaints and nothing changed. The experience didn't erode their motivation. The broken loop did. A fast reporting flow only works if the loop closes on the other end.

The design I built here is a better complaint box, yes. The harder thing it tries to be is a system where complaints lead to verified resolutions. Where citizens confirm fixes. Where fixed things are as visible as broken ones. Design has to lead on that. Someone has to draw what a closed loop looks like before engineering can build one.
